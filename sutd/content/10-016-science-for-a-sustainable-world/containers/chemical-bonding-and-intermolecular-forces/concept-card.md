# Chemical Bonding and Intermolecular Forces

## First-Principles Explanation

Chemical bonds are attractions that hold atoms together inside a particle or lattice. Intermolecular forces are attractions between particles. The distinction matters: a water molecule has strong O-H covalent bonds inside it, but boiling water mainly separates water molecules from each other by overcoming hydrogen bonding between molecules.

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons. A large electronegativity difference supports ionic bonding. A moderate difference supports polar covalent bonding. A very small difference supports nonpolar covalent bonding. Molecular shape then decides whether polar bonds add up to a polar molecule or cancel.

## Why This Matters

Bonding and intermolecular forces explain melting point, boiling point, solubility, plastic behaviour, battery electrolytes, water treatment, and environmental persistence. They connect atomic-scale structure to sustainable-world materials decisions.

## Canonical Example

Sodium chloride has a large electronegativity difference between sodium and chlorine, so it is best modelled as ionic. Water has polar O-H bonds and a bent shape, so separate water molecules attract through hydrogen bonding. Carbon dioxide has polar C=O bonds, but its linear shape cancels the molecular dipole, so London dispersion is the main attraction between molecules.

## Common Misconceptions

- Hydrogen bonding is not the covalent O-H bond inside one water molecule; it is an attraction between molecules.
- Polar bonds do not automatically make a polar molecule. Molecular geometry can cancel bond dipoles.
- Ionic bonding is not a pair of isolated molecules; it is better represented as attraction through an extended lattice.
