# Atomic Structure and Electron Configuration

## First-Principles Explanation

Atomic number is the number of protons in an atom's nucleus. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons, so atomic number also fixes the total electron count for the neutral atom.

Electrons are not placed anywhere at random. In the first-year model, lower-energy shells and subshells fill first. The first shell holds 2 electrons. The second shell can hold 8 electrons in the `2s` and `2p` subshells. The next electrons begin the third shell, which explains why elements in the same column of the periodic table often share similar valence-electron patterns.

Electron configuration is the compact notation for this filling pattern. For carbon, `Z = 6`, so a neutral carbon atom has 6 electrons. The configuration is `1s2 2s2 2p2`, which is often summarized by shells as `2, 4`. The outer shell has 4 valence electrons.

## Why This Matters

Valence electrons are the bridge from atomic structure to bonding, reactivity, materials, batteries, water treatment, and semiconductor band theory. If the electron count or shell model is wrong, later explanations of ions, covalent bonds, metallic bonding, and band gaps become guesswork.

## Canonical Example

Sodium has `Z = 11`, so neutral sodium has 11 electrons. Its configuration is `1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1`, summarized as `2, 8, 1`. The one electron in the outer shell makes it natural to discuss sodium as an atom that can lose one electron when forming an ion.

## Common Misconceptions

- Atomic mass is not the same as atomic number. Mass includes protons and neutrons; neutral electron count follows proton count.
- The first shell does not hold every electron. Shell capacities force electrons into higher shells.
- A full inner shell is not the same as a full outer shell. Chemical behaviour depends strongly on the outer, valence shell.
