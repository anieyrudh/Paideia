# Physical Quantities and Units

## First-Principles Explanation

A physical quantity is something about the world that can be measured. A useful
measurement gives the quantity, a numerical value, a unit, and — when the
reading comes from an instrument — a sensible uncertainty. Writing `18.4` is
only a number; writing `18.4 ± 0.3 cm` says the quantity is a length, the unit
is centimetre, and repeated readings limit how confidently the value is known.

Units are not decoration. They carry the dimension of the quantity. You can
change `12.6 cm` to `0.126 m` without changing the physical length, but you
cannot change it to `12.6 s` without changing the kind of quantity. The same
unit discipline also rejects impossible equations before any numbers are
substituted.

## Key Definitions

- **Physical quantity:** a measurable property such as length, time, mass, or force.
- **Numerical value:** how many units are counted.
- **Unit:** the agreed standard used for comparison.
- **Uncertainty:** the stated limit on a measurement caused by instrument resolution, repeated-reading spread, or both.
- **Base quantity:** a quantity such as length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, or luminous intensity.
- **Derived quantity:** a quantity built from base quantities, such as speed in `m s^-1` or force in `kg m s^-2`.
- **Dimensionally consistent equation:** an equation where both sides reduce to the same base dimensions.

## Why This Matters

Every later physics calculation depends on this layer. Units catch impossible
answers, show whether two quantities can be added, and expose when a formula has
been used incorrectly. Uncertainty keeps the calculator from pretending that a
measured value is more precise than the experiment supports.

## Canonical Examples

- Distance may be measured as `2.0 m`, `200 cm`, or `0.002 km`; the physical length is the same.
- A track length reported as `18.4 ± 0.3 cm` separates the best estimate from the uncertainty.
- Speed has units `m s^-1` because it is distance divided by time.
- `speed = length ÷ time` is dimensionally consistent; `speed = length × time` is not.
- Acceleration has units `m s^-2`, not `m s^-1`, because velocity changes per unit time.

## Common Misconceptions

- Treating units as labels added after the calculation.
- Thinking the number alone is the measurement.
- Quoting every calculator digit while ignoring measurement uncertainty.
- Adding unlike quantities because the numerical values look compatible.

## Problem-Solving Pattern

1. Name the physical quantity.
2. Write the numerical value with its unit.
3. State the uncertainty from repeated readings or instrument resolution.
4. Reduce derived units to base units if needed.
5. Check whether operations preserve dimensions.
6. Convert units only by multiplying by a ratio equal to one.
