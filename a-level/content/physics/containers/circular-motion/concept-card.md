# Circular Motion

## First-Principles Explanation

Uniform circular motion has constant speed but changing velocity because the direction of motion changes continuously. The acceleration is radial and points toward the centre of the circle, so the resultant force required for the motion also points toward the centre.

## What Learners Should Be Able To Do

- Distinguish speed from velocity in a circular path.
- Use `a_c = v^2 / r` to calculate radial acceleration with SI units.
- Use `F_c = m a_c = m v^2 / r` to calculate the inward resultant force.
- Identify the real force that supplies the inward resultant force in a context, such as friction for a car on a flat bend or gravity for orbital motion.
- Predict how increasing speed, decreasing radius, or increasing mass changes the required inward force.

## Core Relationships

```text
a_c = v^2 / r
F_c = m a_c = m v^2 / r
omega = v / r
T = 2 pi r / v
```

where `v` is speed in m s^-1, `r` is radius in m, `m` is mass in kg, `a_c` is radial acceleration in m s^-2, `F_c` is the inward resultant force in N, `omega` is angular speed in rad s^-1, and `T` is period in s.

## Misconception Map

- Constant speed means zero acceleration: speed is constant, but velocity direction changes, so acceleration is non-zero.
- Centripetal force is an extra new force: `F_c` is the inward resultant force; the named real force depends on the physical situation.
- The force points outward: outward sensation belongs to the turning frame; the inertial force diagram needs an inward resultant force.

## Paideia PMOE-T Loop

- Predict: choose the acceleration direction before vectors and readouts appear.
- Manipulate: adjust mass, speed, radius, and position on the circle.
- Observe: compare tangent velocity with inward acceleration and force vectors, with formula substitution beside the visual.
- Explain: connect the vector direction to changing velocity direction.
- Transfer: identify gravity as the inward force in an orbital context.
