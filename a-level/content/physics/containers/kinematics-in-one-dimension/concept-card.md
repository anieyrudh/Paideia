# Kinematics in One Dimension

## First-Principles Explanation

Kinematics describes motion without first asking what force caused it. In one
dimension, every quantity is measured along a chosen line, so signs carry the
direction information.

Displacement is change in position. Velocity is the rate of change of
displacement. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. If acceleration
is constant over a time interval, the velocity-time graph is a straight line,
so displacement is the area under that graph.

For motion along one line with initial velocity `u`, constant acceleration `a`,
elapsed time `t`, final velocity `v`, and displacement `s`:

```text
v = u + at
s = ut + 1/2 at^2
v^2 = u^2 + 2as
```

These are not separate tricks. They are different ways to say that velocity
changes steadily with time and displacement accumulates from velocity.

## Key Definitions

- **Displacement:** signed change in position from start to finish.
- **Velocity:** rate of change of displacement; it includes direction.
- **Acceleration:** rate of change of velocity.
- **Constant acceleration:** acceleration that has the same value throughout the interval.
- **Initial velocity:** velocity at the start of the timed interval.

## Why This Matters

Kinematics is the bridge from measurement and vectors to dynamics. Before a
force can be linked to acceleration, a learner must be able to read motion
graphs, preserve signs, and choose equations using the quantities actually
given in the problem.

## Canonical Examples

- A trolley starting from rest under constant acceleration has increasing velocity, so displacement grows faster than time.
- A lift can move upward while having negative acceleration if it is slowing down.
- A velocity-time graph below the time axis represents displacement in the negative direction.

## Common Misconceptions

- Treating negative acceleration as automatically meaning negative velocity.
- Multiplying final velocity by time even though velocity changed during the interval.
- Forgetting that displacement has a sign while distance is a path length.
- Using a constant-acceleration equation when acceleration is not constant over the interval.

## Problem-Solving Pattern

1. Choose the positive direction and write it down.
2. List the known quantities with units and signs.
3. Decide whether acceleration is constant over the interval.
4. Choose the equation containing the wanted unknown and the known quantities.
5. Substitute with units, then check whether the sign and magnitude make physical sense.
